Ring the changeswhere England's bells hang

Dove's Guide lists {{counts.rings|int}} rings of bells hung for full-circle change ringing, holding {{counts.bells|int}} bells. I mapped every one: where they hang, how heavy they are, how old, who cast them, and the years when England cast the most.

Some time in the early seventeenth century, English ringers found that a bell swung right round, mouth up, and held balanced there for a moment, could be controlled to the split second. A band of ringers could then make the bells strike in a different order at every pull: ringing the changes. By 1668, when Richard Duckworth and Fabian Stedman published Tintinnalogia, it had rules. It is still an almost wholly English art.

Since 1950 ringers have found their towers with Dove's Guide, first compiled by Ronald Dove, who rang at nearly every tower himself. Today the Central Council of Church Bell Ringers publishes it online, with a record for every ring and for {{counts.bell_records|int}} of the bells in them: weight, note, founder and the year each was cast. It is open data, and it is a wonderful record of English parish life. Here is what it shows.

01 · The mapFind your tower

    02 · WhereAn English art, and a southern one

    Of the {{counts.rings|int}} rings, {{counts.england|int}} are in England, {{counts.wales|int}} in Wales, {{counts.ireland|int}} on the island of Ireland and just {{counts.scotland|int}} in Scotland. Within England they thicken towards the south and the Midlands. Devon has the most ({{county_most.0.rings|int}}), then Somerset ({{county_most.1.rings|int}}) and Lincolnshire ({{county_most.2.rings|int}}). Measured by area, the densest large counties are {{county_density_top.0.county}}, {{county_density_top.1.county}} and {{county_density_top.2.county}}, at over nine rings per 100 square kilometres; Northumberland has {{county_density_bottom.3.per100|f2}}.

    A fairer test is to ask what share of churches have a ring. OpenStreetMap maps {{geo.anglican_osm|int}} Anglican churches in England and Wales, and {{geo.anglican_with_ring|int}} of them ({{geo.anglican_share|pct}}) have a Dove ring within 150 metres. In Northamptonshire it is {{county_share_top.0.share|pct}}, in Devon {{county_share_top.1.share|pct}}. In the far north of England and in north and west Wales it falls to between {{county_share_bottom.5.share|pct}} and {{county_share_bottom.0.share|pct}}: there most parish churches have no ring at all.

    Counties: ceremonial counties of England and preserved counties of Wales (ONS boundaries). Churches: OpenStreetMap places of worship tagged Christian and Anglican (Church of England, Church in Wales); churches with no denomination tag are left out, so the shares are approximate.

    The rings are close together. Half of all towers in England and Wales are within {{geo.nn_median_km|f1}} km of another ring, and {{geo.nn_within3|pct}} within 3 km. The loneliest is at Hugh Town on the Isles of Scilly, {{geo.nn_max_km|f1}} km from the nearest ring on the mainland.

    03 · How many bellsSixes, eights and the rise of the twelves

    The typical English ring has six bells: {{size_groups.six|int}} rings ({{size_groups.six_share|pct}}) do. There are {{size_groups.eight|int}} rings of eight, {{size_groups.ten|int}} of ten and {{size_groups.twelve|int}} of twelve. {{size_groups.over12|Words}} towers go beyond twelve: Winchester Cathedral has 14, Ossett 15, and St Martin's in Birmingham, Christ Church Cathedral in Dublin and the Bell Tower in Perth, Western Australia, have 16. Most of the extras are semitone bells, which let a band ring a lighter ten or twelve.

    At the small end there are {{size_groups.small|int}} rings of three to five bells, and they are where the silence is. Dove marks {{unringable.n|int}} rings ({{unringable.share|pct}}) as unringable, and {{unringable.small_share_of_ur|pct}} of those are rings of three, four or five: {{unringable.three_share|pct}} of all rings of three cannot be rung, against {{unringable.six_share|pct1}} of sixes and {{unringable.eight_share|pct1}} of eights.

    Big rings are mostly new. Dove does not say when a ring reached its present size, but it does give the year each bell was cast, and a ring cannot be older than its newest bell. For the rings of twelve, the median newest bell was cast in {{growth_by.12.median_newest|raw}}, and {{growth_by.12.share_since1950|pct}} have at least one bell cast since 1950. For rings of ten the median is {{growth_by.10.median_newest|raw}}; for eights {{growth_by.8.median_newest|raw}}. The twelve-bell ring, the summit of English ringing, is largely a creation of the last seventy years.

    04 · Heavy metalThe tenors

    Ringers weigh bells in hundredweights, quarters and pounds: a hundredweight is 112 lb, about 51 kg. The median tenor, the heaviest bell of a ring, is about {{tenor.median_cwt|raw}} cwt ({{tenor.median_kg|int}} kg). {{tenor.over_ton|int}} rings have a tenor of a ton or more, and {{tenor.over_2ton|int}} have one of two tons or more. The heaviest of all is at Liverpool Cathedral: {{heaviest.0.tenor_cwt}}, about {{heaviest.0.tenor_kg|int}} kg, swung full circle by one ringer on one rope.

    05 · Old metalBells older than the Reformation

    The median bell in a ring was cast in {{ages.median_year|raw}}. But {{ages.before1500|int}} bells still hanging in rings were cast before 1500, most dated by their style rather than an inscription, in {{ages.medieval_rings|int}} different rings. Suffolk has the most ({{ages.medieval_top_counties.0.n|int}}), then Norfolk ({{ages.medieval_top_counties.1.n|int}}), Devon ({{ages.medieval_top_counties.2.n|int}}) and Somerset ({{ages.medieval_top_counties.3.n|int}}). These bells were cast for swing chiming long before anyone rang changes on them.

    The oldest bell with a cast date in Dove's Guide that still hangs in a ring is the fourth of six at {{oldest_dated_first.place}}, {{oldest_dated_first.county}}, dated {{oldest_dated_first.year|raw}} and attributed to {{oldest_dated_first.founder}}.

    06 · The foundersFrom Gloucester to Whitechapel to Loughborough

    Dove names {{founders.n_founders|int}} founders for the bells in rings. Before 1700 they were many and mostly local, each serving a county or two. In the eighteenth century the Rudhalls of Gloucester cast bells by the thousand ({{founders.rudhall|int}} still hang in rings). In the nineteenth, London's Whitechapel foundry and the Taylors of Loughborough took over, joined by Warner of London and Gillett & Johnston of Croydon.

    Between them, Whitechapel (with its earlier owners) and Taylor cast {{founders.big2_share|pct}} of all the bells in rings today, and {{founders.c20_big2_share|pct}} of those cast in the twentieth century. Whitechapel's last bells in the data are dated {{founders.whitechapel_last|raw}}; the foundry closed that year after more than four centuries. Since 2018 Taylor has cast {{founders.since2018_taylor_share|pct}} of new bells in rings.

    07 · Jubilees and warsThe years England cast bells

    Plot the bells by the year on them and the history of the country rings out. The two biggest single years are {{cast_years.top.0.year|raw}}, with {{cast_years.top.0.n|int}} bells, and {{cast_years.top.1.year|raw}}, with {{cast_years.top.1.n|int}}: Queen Victoria's Diamond and Golden Jubilees, each about twice the years around them. The coronation of George VI in 1937 shows the same jump ({{event.1937.n|int}} bells against an average of {{event.1937.base|int}} in the years around it), and so do 1902 and 1911.

    In 1943 and 1944, not one bell that hangs in a ring today was cast.

    The wars are just as clear. From 1915 to 1918 the average fell to {{wars.ww1|int}} a year, against {{wars.pre1|int}} a year in 1910 to 1914. In the Second World War church bells were silenced, to be rung only to warn of invasion: {{wars.ww2_total|int}} bells from 1940 to 1945, {{wars.y1941|int}} in 1941, {{wars.y1942|int}} in 1942 and none at all in 1943 or 1944. In 1946 there were {{wars.y1946|int}}. The busiest decade of all was the {{decades.peak_decade|raw}}s, the decade of the war memorials, with {{decades.peak_n|int}} bells.

    08 · Oak, iron, steelWhat the bells hang in

    A ring of bells swinging in time puts large forces on a tower, and the frame that holds them matters as much as the bells. Before 1850, {{frames.timber_share_pre1850|pct}} of the frames still in use were timber, mostly oak. From the 1870s cast iron took over ({{frames.iron_share_1875_1950|pct}} of frames from 1875 to 1949), and since 1975 most new frames are steel ({{frames.steel_share_since1975|pct}}).

    09 · Practice nightTuesday, mostly

    {{practice.listed|int}} rings list a weekly practice night in Dove, {{practice.listed_share_ringable|pct}} of the ringable ones. {{practice.midweek_share|pct}} are on a Monday to Thursday, with Tuesday just ahead. Hardly anyone practises at the weekend, when the bells are rung for services and weddings. {{extras.ground_floor|int}} rings ({{extras.ground_floor_share|pct}}) are rung from the ground floor, with no ladder to climb, and {{extras.simulator|int}} have a simulator that lets a band practise silently.

    Practice nights
    Rings by the first practice day listed in Dove's Guide.

    Most of these rings hang in listed buildings. {{extras.grade1_share_eng|pct}} of the rings in England are in a Grade I listed building, and {{extras.listed_share_eng|pct}} are in a listed building of some grade. The commonest dedication is to St Mary ({{dedications.mary|int}} English rings, {{dedications.mary_share|pct}}), then All Saints ({{dedications.all_saints|int}}) and St Peter alone ({{dedications.peter|int}}). Mary leads among all the churches of the United Kingdom too, with {{saints.mary_share|pct}} in my saints and wells study, where All Saints came fourth.

    10 · Beyond EnglandThe world's rings

    {{abroad.n|int}} rings are outside the British Isles, in {{abroad.countries|int}} countries.     How I did this

    Data. Dove's Guide for Church Bell Ringers, downloaded from the Central Council of Church Bell Ringers on {{counts.retrieved_text}}: the rings file ({{counts.rings|int}} rings of three or more bells hung for full-circle ringing, {{counts.lightweight|int}} of them lightweight or mini rings), the bells file ({{counts.bell_records|int}} records for bells in those rings, leaving out sanctus and service bells), and the founders and frames files. A tower with two rings counts twice.

    Cast years. Dove gives a cast date for {{ages.with_year|int}} bells. A single year (sometimes in brackets) counts as dated; "c" dates count as approximate at the year given; a range counts at its midpoint. The year charts use dated bells only; ages and the founders chart use all. Many medieval bells carry Dove's style dates such as c1499, so "before 1500" means "dated or judged to be medieval".

    Founders. Foundry families follow the Group column in Dove's founders table: Whitechapel includes the Mears, Lester, Pack, Chapman and Stainbank names; Taylor includes the Taylors of St Neots, Oxford and Loughborough.

    Places. Counties by point in polygon with ONS boundaries (ceremonial counties in England, preserved counties in Wales). Churches from OpenStreetMap; a church has a ring if a Dove ring lies within 150 m. Distances between towers on the British National Grid.

    Limits. Dove records rings that exist now. Bells recast, sold or melted down are gone from it, so the record of past centuries is a record of survival. Dates of the newest bell are an upper bound on when a ring took its present form, not the date of augmentation. Practice nights are as towers report them and change often; check with the tower before you go.
